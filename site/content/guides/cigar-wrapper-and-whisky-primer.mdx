The wrapper leaf drives most of a cigar's flavor and strength — more than the filler inside it. If you learn to read three wrapper types, you can predict roughly what any cigar will taste like before you light it.

## The three wrappers that matter

**Connecticut Shade** is pale and thin-veined, grown under cheesecloth tents in the Connecticut River Valley and Ecuador. It's creamy, mild, and faintly sweet — a reliable starting point for a first cigar.

**Habano** is medium-brown, originally Cuban seed and now mostly grown in Nicaragua and Ecuador. Expect leather, pepper, and a firmer draw — a step up in intensity without being difficult.

**Maduro** is dark and oily from extra fermentation or fire-curing, which converts starches into sugar. It's sweet, roasted, and full-bodied — the wrapper aficionados tend to reach for.

## Matching a whisky style

The same ladder of intensity applies to whisky. **Bourbon** — American, at least 51% corn, aged in new charred oak — brings vanilla, caramel, and corn sweetness that plays well with a Connecticut wrapper. **Scotch** varies by region: Speyside runs fruity and approachable, while Islay runs peated and smoky, which stands up to a Maduro. **Irish and Canadian whiskies**, typically triple-distilled or blended, are the most forgiving starting point if you're pairing for a first-timer.

## Why a cigar changes as it burns

A cigar's flavor shifts across three stages. The **first third** is mildest, as the cigar warms up. The **midpoint** is the fullest flavor, with a steady burn. The **final third** is the boldest and warmest, and often a favorite. That's why a tasting card built around a single cigar pours a fresh whisky at each stage, lightest first — rather than working through three separate cigars in one sitting, which is more smoke than most nights actually need.

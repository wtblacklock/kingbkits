The single easiest upgrade to any tasting night costs nothing and takes no extra supplies: make people guess before they find out what they're drinking. It sounds like a small change. It changes the entire room.

## Why the order matters

Once someone reads a label, their opinion is already contaminated. They'll taste "expensive" or "cheap" instead of tasting the glass in front of them. Reveal first and every note afterward is really a note about the price tag or the brand, not the liquid. Guess first and you get honest reactions, which are funnier, more useful, and the actual reason people remember the night.

## The format

1. **Pour before anyone sees the bottle.** Whoever is pouring keeps the bottle out of sight, or wraps the label if you're doing a full blind round.
2. **Guess before you taste.** Have each guest write down one or two guesses about what's in the glass: a region, a style, an age range, whatever your kit's tasting card asks for. This is the step people skip and the step that makes it work.
3. **Taste, then commit.** Nose it, sip it, note it. Only after everyone has written something down does anyone say a word out loud.
4. **Reveal, then compare.** Read the label. Compare it against what everyone guessed. This is where the real conversation happens, and it's infinitely better than a room full of people nodding at whatever the first person said.

## Where this idea comes from

Our [24 Nights of Whisky](/kits/whisky-advent) kit is built entirely around this mechanic: every one of the 24 tasting cards asks for a blind guess on region and age *before* the reveal, because that's how real whisky advent calendars work: you don't know what's behind the door until you open it. We liked the format enough to recommend it for any tasting night, calendar or not.

## Keeping it from feeling like a test

Blind tasting can feel intimidating if you frame it as a quiz. Frame it as a bet instead: everyone commits to a guess, the room finds out together who was closest, and the person who guessed worst has to pour the next round. Nobody's wrong answer needs to be right. The gap between the guess and the reveal is the entertainment, not the failure.
